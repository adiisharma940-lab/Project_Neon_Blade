Kawasaki didn't build the H2R to win races — they built it to redefine what a motorcycle could physically be. Track-only. Supercharged. A 998cc inline-four producing 310 horsepower in ram-air conditions, wrapped in carbon fibre winglets and fed by the first centrifugal supercharger ever fitted to a series-production bike. This is not transport. This is controlled detonation.

Supercharger
FORCED
INDUCTION
Kawasaki's engineers designed the supercharger entirely in-house — a centrifugal compressor with nine impeller blades machined to aerospace tolerances. It spins at up to 130,000 RPM, force-feeding the engine a dense wall of compressed air that naturally-aspirated machines can only dream of. The result is a torque curve with no dip, no lag, no mercy — just linear, relentless power from idle to the 14,000 RPM redline.

Aerodynamics
LIFT IS
THE ENEMY
At 300 km/h, air is no longer invisible — it becomes a physical force that can lift the front wheel and pitch a rider into oblivion. Kawasaki's wind-tunnel engineers spent thousands of hours shaping the H2R's carbon fibre body to fight that force at every corner. The dual upper winglets and lower splitter generate enough downforce to keep both tyres planted through corners that would send any other machine airborne. Form follows physics.

The Legend
H2R
FOREVER
In September 2016, Guy Martin strapped himself to a Kawasaki Ninja H2R on the Straightliners top-speed course and rode it to 331.15 km/h — a British land speed record. Not a prototype. Not a factory special. A production machine you can order from a dealership, if you have the nerve and the track permit. No other road-derived motorcycle has ever gone faster. This is where engineering ends and mythology begins.
