Heritage · F1 Bloodline · 2024
WHERE THE
F1 LEFT OFF
The McLaren W1 is not the successor to the P1. It is the successor to the F1 — the 1992 Gordon Murray masterpiece that was the fastest production car on earth for a decade. The W1 carries that lineage deliberately: a central driving position, an engine developed by McLaren's own powertrain team, and an obsessive commitment to the idea that a road car should feel like nothing else has ever felt.
Thirty-two years separate the F1 and the W1. In that time McLaren learned to win Formula 1 championships, survived near bankruptcy, built the P1 and the Senna and the Speedtail, and emerged with something rare in the hypercar world: genuine engineering conviction. The W1 is what happens when a company stops trying to compete and starts trying to be correct.

Powertrain · Hybrid · E-Turbo
THE HEART
IS ELECTRIFIED
FURY
At the centre of the W1 is a 4.0-litre twin-turbocharged V8 — a clean-sheet design by McLaren Automotive's own powertrain engineers, not a unit shared with any other vehicle. It produces 727 horsepower alone. But McLaren didn't stop there. They fitted the turbocharger shafts with electric motors — a technology borrowed directly from Formula 1 — creating what they call an E-Turbo system.
The electric motors eliminate turbo lag entirely. Before the exhaust gases have even begun to spool the compressor, the electric motor has already spun it to operating speed. There is no delay. There is no step. There is just torque — 900Nm — available from the moment you ask for it, from idle all the way to the 8,500 RPM redline.
A second electric motor drives the rear axle directly, adding 201 horsepower of electric thrust for a combined system output of 928 horsepower. The battery is a 1.196 kWh unit — deliberately small, because the W1 is not an EV. It is a combustion engine with electric supercharging and an instantaneous torque-fill system. Every joule is a weapon.

Aerodynamics · Active · Downforce
AIR IS
SCULPTED
NOT MANAGED
McLaren has always been suspicious of wings. Big fixed wings are a lazy solution — they generate downforce but also drag, and drag is the enemy of speed. The W1 takes a different philosophy: every surface generates downforce, and every surface is active. The car reshapes itself in real time, driven by algorithms that read road conditions, speed, and driver inputs 1,000 times per second.
The front underbody features a Ground Effect Tunnel — a venturi channel that accelerates air under the car, creating a low-pressure zone that literally sucks the W1 to the road. At 150 mph, the W1 generates 1,000 kg of downforce — more than the car weighs. At that moment it would drive on the ceiling. This is not a metaphor. This is physics being exploited by engineers who refused to accept compromise.
An active rear wing adjusts its angle continuously, working in concert with an active front splitter and adjustable ride height. In Race mode, the suspension drops, the aerodynamics tighten, and the W1 transforms from a grand tourer into something that shares more DNA with an LMP1 prototype than anything sold by a road car manufacturer.

The W1 · 106 Built · Sold Out
W1
THE LAST
ARGUMENT
McLaren built 106 examples of the W1. Every one was allocated before the public announcement. The people who own them understand something that numbers alone cannot communicate — that this car represents the closing argument of a company that spent three decades proving that a small British manufacturer could build cars that embarrassed Ferrari, Porsche, and Bugatti on their best days.
The W1 has a carbon fibre MonoCell III-T tub — the third generation of McLaren's signature chassis technology, stiffened specifically for the W1's increased power and downforce loads. The body panels are carbon fibre. The roof is carbon fibre. The floor is carbon fibre. The car is, at its core, a structure so rigid that its torsional stiffness exceeds many dedicated racing cars.
It costs £2.1 million before options. It goes 0–200 mph in under 15 seconds — a figure that puts it in a category with machines that cost twice as much. But price is not the point. The point is that every W1 owner knows they own the closest thing to a racing car that has ever been granted a number plate. That knowledge has no market value. It is simply true.
