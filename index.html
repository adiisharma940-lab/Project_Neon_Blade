Kawasaki didn't build the Ninja H2R to win races — they built it to redefine what a motorcycle could physically be. Born of a collaboration between Kawasaki's Aerospace Company, Gas Turbine & Machinery Company, and the Motorcycle & Engine Company, this was not product development. It was a declaration of intent.
The heart is a 998cc liquid-cooled inline-four with a bore and stroke of 76 × 55mm — a short-stroke, high-revving configuration designed to scream to 14,000 RPM without flinching. In ram-air conditions at speed, the engine produces 310 horsepower — figures that shatter the imagination when you consider it weighs just 216 kg dry.

Supercharger · Centrifugal · In-House
FORCED
INDUCTION
REDEFINED
No motorcycle manufacturer had ever fitted a centrifugal supercharger to a series-production bike before the H2R. Kawasaki didn't outsource the engineering — they designed every component internally, drawing on decades of aerospace turbomachinery experience from their own gas turbine division.
The impeller is a nine-blade centrifugal compressor, each blade individually machined to tolerances measured in microns. At peak demand it spins at 130,000 RPM — over 2,100 revolutions every second — compressing the incoming air charge and ramming it into the intake at pressures far beyond what any naturally-aspirated engine can achieve. The result is a boost pressure of 2.4 bar absolute at peak, with no turbo lag, no step in the powerband, no hesitation. Just a relentless, linear surge that begins the moment you crack the throttle and does not stop until the rev limiter intervenes at 14,000 RPM.
A gear-driven intermediate shaft sits between the crankshaft and compressor, multiplying rotational speed with surgical precision. Intake air temperature is managed by an intercooler integrated into the airbox, preventing heat soak from robbing the charge of density. The entire system weighs less than 3 kg — a masterpiece of miniaturised engineering.

Aerodynamics · Wind Tunnel · Downforce
LIFT IS
THE ENEMY
OF SPEED
At 300 km/h, aerodynamics are not a styling exercise — they are a survival calculation. The Ninja H2R's bodywork was developed in Kawasaki's own wind tunnel, with engineers spending thousands of hours perfecting every surface. The objective: generate maximum downforce without adding drag, keeping the front wheel pinned to the track at speeds where aerodynamic lift would otherwise launch the machine skyward.
The dual upper winglets — sculpted in carbon fibre reinforced polymer — generate downforce that acts directly on the fork assembly, pressing the front tyre into the asphalt with every additional kilometre per hour. A lower chin splitter further manipulates the underfloor airflow, reducing lift at the front axle. At 200 km/h, the H2R generates enough downforce to meaningfully alter its suspension behaviour — the bike gets lower as it gets faster.
The ram-air intake system is a dual function: it feeds the supercharger with a high-pressure air charge at speed while simultaneously acting as a structural aerodynamic element. Every vent, every louver, every gap in the bodywork has a specific thermodynamic or aerodynamic purpose. Nothing is decorative. Nothing is wasted.

The Legend · 331.15 km/h · 2016
H2R
FOREVER
UNTAMED
In September 2016, Guy Martin — television presenter, truck mechanic, and one of the fastest motorcycle racers Britain has ever produced — strapped himself to a Kawasaki Ninja H2R on the Straightliners top-speed event at Elvington Airfield and rode it to 331.15 km/h (205.8 mph). That number is a British motorcycle land speed record. It remains unbroken.
What makes this record staggering is context. This was not a factory prototype with a one-off engine. It was not a streamliner fairing built specifically for speed runs. It was a production motorcycle — a machine that Kawasaki dealers around the world will sell you today, assuming you have the nerve and a track permit. The H2R has no road registration, no headlights, no number plate bracket. It exists purely for circuits and for moments like Elvington.
The Kawasaki Ninja H2R is the only motorcycle in history to have been built by the same company that makes fighter jet components, helicopter transmissions, and industrial gas turbines — and it shows. Every system on this machine reflects engineering culture forged in environments where failure is not an option. It is not the fastest vehicle ever made. But it is the most extraordinary motorcycle that has ever existed.
